Diamond can also be an outstanding semiconductor material, 
superior in many ways to silicon. Diamond devices could operate at 
higher temperatures (>400 C) and higher power than those of 
silicon, as well as being faster, denser and more resistant to 
radiation. But practical diamond electronics will need large-area, 
single-crystalline diamond wafers to be fabricated, analogous to 
silicon wafers commonly used in the semiconductor industry.
Diamond can be grown on diamond (homoepitaxy) by chemical 
vapor deposition. Electronics-grade diamond has been made in this 
way. But single-crystal diamond substrates are small and expensive.
The alternative is to grow diamond on a foreign (non-diamond) 
single-crystal wafer (heteroepitaxy).

This course will focus on the deposition of thin films from the 
vapor phase.
We will not discuss the deposition of thin films from the liquid 
phase or films grown by reaction with the substrate rather than 
by deposition, such as SiO2 grown by the thermal reaction of 
water vapor with Si.
Vapor phase thin-film techniques have three advantages: (1) 
applicability to any material, (2) wide adjustability in substrate 
temperature, and (3) access to the surface during deposition.
Liquid-phase techniques, such as electroplating, are useful for 
low-cost production. They will not be covered in this course.

Process Steps
Solid materials are vaporized by heat 
or by an energetic beam of electrons, 
photons (laser ablation), or positively 
ions (sputtering) in order to transport 
them to substrates. These methods 
are categorized as physical vapor 
deposition (PVD). Thin-film 
processes that use gases, 
evaporating liquids, or chemically 
gasified solids as source materials 
are categorized as chemical vapor 
deposition (CVD).
The key difference is that PVD is a 
physical transfer from solid-to-
solid (through vapor), while CVD 
is a chemical reaction from gas-
to-solid (often with flow control).


=== PAGE 36 ===
36
A major issue in the transport step is the uniformity of the arrival rate over the 
substrate. In a high vacuum, molecules travel from the source to the substrate in 
straight lines, whereas in a fluid there are many molecular collisions during the 
transport step. Therefore, in a high vacuum, the uniformity is determined by the 
geometry, whereas in a fluid it is determined by gas flow patterns and by the 
diffusion of source molecules.
Substrate surface conditions include roughness, contamination level, degree of 
chemical bonding with arriving materials, and crystallographic parameters.
The reactivity factor refers to the probability of arriving molecules reacting with the 
surface and becoming incorporated into the film. The probability is known as the 
“sticking coefficient”, Sc, which can vary from 1.0 to <10‒3.
Energy input, including substrate heating, energetic electrons, photons, plasma, 
chemical reactions, has profound effects on both the reactivity of arriving materials 
and on the composition and structure of films.
In physics, a fluid is any substance that continuously deforms or flows when a 
shear stress is applied. It includes liquids, gases, and even plasmas.
