At very low kinetic energies (a few eV above the Fermi level),  many states are not available in solid for inelastic excitations , so the inelastic scattering probability is relatively low and the mean free path is comparatively large.
Between roughly 10–100 eV, the electron energy matches well the typical excitation energies of valence electrons and plasmons in solids, so there are many available final states and allowed transitions.
As kinetic energy rises further (hundreds of eV to keV and beyond), the electron moves faster and spends less time interacting with the same region of the medium, which effectively reduces the probability of inelastic collision per unit distance.

"Bremsstrahlung" describes the radiation which is emitted when electrons are decelerated when they are fired at a metal target. During the deceleration, the charged particles give off electromagnetic radiation. When the energy of the bombarding electrons is high enough, that radiation is in the x-ray region. It is characterized by a continuous distribution of radiation which becomes more intense and shifts toward higher frequencies when the energy of the bombarding electrons is increased.

Electron multiplier
An electron multiplier consists of a series of electrodes called 
dynodes
.
The dynode potentials differ in equal steps along the chain. 
 A high-voltage gradient (typically hundreds to thousands of volts) is applied across the dynodes, 
so each subsequent dynode has a more positive potential than the previous one
.
When a particle (electron, ion, high energy neutral, or high energy photon) strikes the first dynode it produces secondary electrons. The secondary electrons are accelerated into the next dynode where each electron produces more secondary electrons.
A 
cascade of secondary electrons 
is generated. The dynode acceleration potential controls the electron gain.

Microchannel plate (MCP)
XPS system: Electron Detector-MCP
A micro-channel plate is a slab made from highly resistive material of typically 2 mm thickness with a regular array of tiny tubes leading from one face to the opposite, densely distributed over the whole surface.
The microchannels are typically approximately 10 um in diameter (6 um in high resolution MCPs) and spaced apart by approximately 15 um; they are parallel to each other and often enter the plate at a small angle to the surface (~8° from normal). 
Each microchannel is a continuous-

[Speaker notes]
In X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS), chemical shifts in core-level binding energies arise from changes in the local chemical environment of atoms. These shifts include initial-state effects (like charge redistribution and orbital energies) and final-state effects, where the latter encompass relaxation and screening processes after photoionization.
The relaxation effect, a key final-state contribution, occurs when valence electrons and surrounding atoms rapidly reorganize to screen the sudden core-hole created by photoelectron ejection, stabilizing the ion and reducing the measured binding energy.
relaxation and final-state effects are particularly crucial for transition metals due to their partially filled d-orbitals, which enable exceptional electron screening of the core-hole. This d-electron participation provides substantial extra-atomic relaxation energy, significantly reducing observed binding energy shifts and masking pure initial-state chemical trends.

Zalar rotation is a specialized sample rotation technique widely utilized in surface analysis technologies like XPS and Auger Electron Spectroscopy (AES) during ion beam sputtering depth profiling. It is designed to address artifacts arising from stationary ion bombardment and enhance the accuracy of depth profile analysis. 
In the common ion bombardment process, surface artifacts are generated due to crystal orientation dependence and ion shadowing effects. Zalar rotation makes the sample surface receive ion bombardment from all azimuth angles. This balances the sputtering effect across the surface, inhibits the formation of local uneven structures, and keeps the sputtered surface flat.

=== PAGE 91 ===
X-Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS), also know as Electron Spectroscopy for Chemical Analysis (ESCA)
,  is a quantitative spectroscopic technique that measures the elemental composition, chemical state and electronic state of the elements that exist within a material. 
XPS spectra are obtained by
 irradiating a material with a beam of X-rays
 while simultaneously 
measuring the kinetic energy and number of electrons
 that escape from the top 1 to 10 nm of the material being analyzed.
Electrons originating from the 
core levels
 identify the elements present from their binding energies (BE). Small chemical shifts in the BEs provide additional chemical state information. The relative concentrations of the different elements present can be determined from the relative peak intensities.
Summary Notes
91

=== PAGE 92 ===
XPS identifies all elements except H and He, because the diameter of these orbitals is so small, reducing the catch probability to almost zero. 
The strengths of XPS are its 
good quantification, its excellent chemical state determination capabilities, its applicability to a wide variety of materials from biological materials to metals, and its generally nondestructive nature.
The weaknesses are its 
lack of good spatial resolution, only moderate absolute sensitivity, and its inability to detect H.
XPS is routinely used to analyze 
inorganic compounds, metal alloys, semiconductors, polymers, elements, catalysts, glasses, ceramics, paints, papers, inks, woods, plant parts, make-up, teeth, bones, medical implants, bio-materials, viscous oils, glues, ion modified materials
 and many others. 
 XPS requires ultra high vacuum (UHV) conditions.
