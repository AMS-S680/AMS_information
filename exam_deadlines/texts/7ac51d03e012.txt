3.1 Thermodynamics of Evaporation
We first consider the vapor-liquid or vapor-solid 
equilibrium situation. Here a single element or a pure 
compound that does not dissociate upon evaporation is 
considered. Our objective is to predict the evaporation 
rate of a material from the available data such as the 
boiling point and heat of evaporation.
At equilibrium, the pressure in the vapor phase is the 
(saturation) vapor pressure, pv. The equilibrium is a 
dynamic process in which the molar condensation rate, 
Qc, is equal to the molar evaporation rate, Qv.
The system is closed. No mass crosses its boundaries.
Energy in the form of heat, q, can be added, causing evaporation.
The energy in the form of mechanical work, w = pvV, is removed as the vapor 
pushes back the surrounding atmosphere.
Not all of the heat is transformed into work. Most of it is consumed in increasing 
the energy of the molecules within the system, which is called the internal energy, 
U. The internal energy includes the kinetic energy and potential energy of the 
molecules.

3.4 Compounds
Compounds behave differently from alloys during evaporation. Some compounds 
evaporate as molecules that have the composition of the condensed phase, such as 
alkali halide (KI) and alkaline-earth halides (MgF2). Oxides can vary from no 
dissociation to partial dissociation to complete dissociation.
Sometimes reduction from a higher to a lower oxide 
occurs during evaporation. For example, SiO2 evaporates 
as SiO in the presence of a reducing agent (Si, C, H2). 
Also falling into the category of partial dissociation are the 
group IV-VI compounds. For example, PbTe evaporates 
mostly as PbTe, but partly as Pb + (1/2) Te2.
III-V and II-VI semiconductors completely dissociate 
during evaporation. Their vapors usually consist of 
atoms of the metallic element and dimers (Y2) of the 
nonmetallic element. The existence of MY is usually 
indicated by a vertical line at x = 0.5, but in fact there 
is always a narrow range of x over which the 
compound can exist as a single phase. This range is 
known as the single-phase field.

104. Because it is difficult to chemically determine such small changes in 
stoichiometry, the positions of the phase boundaries are unknown for most 
compounds. The compound accommodates nonstoichiometry within the 
single phase by generating native point defects within its crystal lattice.

3.5 Sources
Basic designs
A filament is a single- or multiple-strand W wire 
bent into a shape that suits a particular power 
electrode spacing and mounting. The evaporant
material is deposited on the wire by melting or 
from a salt solution. When sufficient current 
passes through the wire, the material evaporates. 
The filament source is used only for single 
materials and is typically used once.
Filaments
A boat is usually made of W, Ta, or Mo foil and is 
designed to meet mounting or deposition pattern 
requirements. Its shape can be a simple flat sheet 
with a dimple to hold the source or one that 
resembles top hats, inverted top hats, canoes, 
kayaks, troughs, etc. The boat usually has notches at 
either side of the dimple. The resistance at the 
notches is higher, causing those areas to be hotter 
than other parts. With judicious choice of notch sizes, 
the conductive thermal losses to the ends can be 
reduced giving a more uniform temperature along the 
length of the dimple.
Boats

amounts of evaporant than can be placed on a

coiled to fit the crucible’s outside dimensions, 
the evaporant’s upper temperature is somewhat 
limited. Nevertheless, for evaporants with a high

good method of providing adequate material.
Crucible (made of AlOx, BN,

Because of the exponential dependence of pv on T, T control needs to be very 
good. Typically, pv increases about 20 percent per 1 percent rise in evaporant
T (dp/p ~ 20dT/T). Crucible sources provide the best T uniformity and control, 
especially if they are well designed.

Contamination
General consideration: degrease all materials, 
minimize surface areas, avoid volatile constituents, 
and employ baking.
For crucibles, the surface area is minimized by using nonporous grades 
(“pyrolytic grade”) of ceramic for crucibles. Much of the volatile contaminants 
can be removed from crucibles and associated components by “firing” them at 
a higher T than the desired evaporation T, prior to filling the crucible with 
evaporant.
For evaporant materials, chemical analysis should be obtained to determine if 
there are contaminants that will be harmful to the film being deposited. In 
general, only metallic impurities are reported by manufacturers. That is, a 
99.99% pure material will have <0.01% (100 ppm) total metallic impurity 
content, but it might also contain a great amount of O, C, Cl, etc. The 
evaporant should be obtained in large pieces rather than in powdered form to 
minimize the surface area. The amount of outgassing of adsorbed water from 
large surface areas is considerable.

26


=== PAGE 27 ===
27
Much of the volatile impurity content in the evaporant can be removed 
before film deposition. This includes adsorbed gases and also dissolved 
elements of higher pv than the evaporant. Removal is accomplished by 
“soaking” the source at a T somewhat below the T for film deposition, both 
before substrate loading and again afterwards while blocking the source 
from the substrate. The second soak removes water adsorbed during the 
loading process.
The dissolved impurities having higher pv will progressively deplete relative 
to the evaporant. Conversely, impurities with pv significantly less than that 
of the evaporant will selectively remain in the crucible. As the evaporant
charge becomes depleted, the fraction of low-pv impurities in it increases. 
Therefore, it is unwise to evaporate the source material to completion 
when the film purity is important.
Impurities with pv similar to that of the evaporant cannot be selectively 
removed and will remain part of the evaporation flux.

Temperature control
To maintain a constant crucible temperature, the power supply for the heater 
is driven by a feedback control loop which compares the thermocouple voltage 
with a set-point voltage. The simplest kind of T control is “on-off”. The 
heater power is fully on when T is below the set point and fully off when it is 
above, so that T continues to oscillate about the set point. For most thin-film 
applications, this type of control is inadequate.
A more sophisticated type of T control is the “PID” type. It has three 
modes of control: proportional, integral or “reset”, and derivative or “rate”. If 
the thermocouple T is lower than the set point by T, the proportional control 
signal driving the power supply is a fraction of “full on”. With proportional 
control alone, the power will remain “on” enough to maintain steady T only if 
there remains a finite offset T from the set point. The integral control mode 
gradually removes this offset by adding a control signal. Finally, the derivative 
mode reduces T overshoot past the set point on initial heat-up by adding a 
third control signal proportional to the minus of the rate of approach of T to the 
set point.

Energy enhancement
In many thin-film deposition processes, energy input to the film surface is 
required in order to increase the reaction rate of the depositing species with 
each other or with the substrate, or to help the depositing species move 
around on the surface.
In thermal evaporation, the energy content of the evaporant is much smaller 
than typical chemical bond energies, so it does not have much effect on film 
growth.


=== PAGE 38 ===
38
There are many ways to enhance evaporant energy so as to modify film 
growth: laser ablation, sputtering, glow-discharge plasma, etc. We consider 
the supersonic nozzle below.
The downstream pressure is 
neglected. In the molecular flow 
regime (Kn > 1), the throughput Qe
(molecules/s) upstream p. In the 
fluid-flow regime (Kn << 1), Qe p2. 
One factor of p comes from the vapor 
concentration increase with p. The 
second factor comes from the fact that 
the fluid flow velocity through an orifice 
increases with the p driving force.
From the molecular viewpoint, collisions between molecules in the movement 
to the orifice serve to convert random thermal translational energy into 
directed kinetic energy moving downstream. Downstream of the orifice, 
further acceleration into the supersonic regime is enhanced by incorporating 
a conical nozzle. Typical parameters for thin film deposition would be a p of 3 
atm, an orifice diameter of 0.3 mm, and a cone angle of 15.


=== PAGE 39 ===
39
Two characteristics of supersonic nozzles are important. One is that considerable 
vapor cooling takes place due to the transfer of kinetic energy from thermal to 
directed. Cooling is efficient for two reasons: (1) Because u is so high, little heat is 
received from the orifice wall. The expansion is adiabatic; (2) For an optimized 
cone angle, there are negligible friction losses, which would transfer kinetic 
energy back to thermal energy.
The second arises from the fact that all components of a gas mixture get 
accelerated in any fluid flow regime. This results in a large flow of the carrier gas, 
which must be pumped away. Most of the pumping is done between the nozzle 
and skimmer. The skimmer serves both to isolate the deposition chamber from the 
higher-p region and to deflect the carrier gas out of the supersonic stream to 
minimize collisional losses.

3.6 Transport
The main issues in the first step, the supply of the source material, are 
the supply rate and contamination. The second step is the transport of 
the source material to the substrate, where the main issue is the 
arrival-rate uniformity at the substrate.
The uniformity depends on whether the process is operated in the 
molecular-flow or fluid-flow regime. We will examine transport in the 
molecular-flow regime with Kn > 1. The characteristic dimension L is 
the distance from the source to substrate. Under this condition, 
transport is along the line of sight from the source to substrate.
Consequently, the arrival-rate uniformity across the substrate is 
determined solely by geometrical factors.

3.7 Vapor Flux Monitoring
It is often difficult to predict accurately the flux arriving at the substrate, due to uncertainty in 
the evaporation rates of various species or due to the presence of lobed flux distributions. Flux 
monitoring is therefore needed. Flux can be monitored either as the concentration of the 
source vapor near the substrate or as the actual deposition rate. Monitoring both is especially 
useful, because one can then calculate the sticking coefficient, Sc.
The simplest type of flux monitors is the 
ion gauge. The ionization rate and resulting

concentration of vapor molecules within 
the gauge, and the flux of any stream of 
particles is proportional to the 
concentration (𝐽𝑖=

Species-specific monitors can be made by

3.8 Deposition Monitoring
Deposition flux is the vapor flux arriving at the substrate times the 
sticking coefficient, Sc. There are several approaches to the deposition 
flux monitoring.
(1) Mass deposition on a vibrating quartz wafer adjacent to the 
substrate;
(2) Change in the optical reflection intensity or phase from the film 
itself with increasing thicknesses;
(3) Periodicity in the reflection high-energy electron diffraction 
(RHEED) signal from epitaxial films.
All of these three approaches are extremely sensitive, with sub-
monolayer resolution being achieved routinely. It is important to note 
that none of them measures the film thickness directly. The thickness 
must be calculated by knowing a film property: (1) density, (2) 
refractive index, and (3) monomolecular (monolayer) thickness.

Quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) is one of the most widely used 
instruments in thin-film technology. It uses crystalline quartz wafers 
that were developed for frequency control in radios, computers, and 
watches. Crystalline quartz is piezoelectric, so a quartz wafer generates 
an oscillating voltage across itself when vibrating at its resonant 
frequency. This voltage can be amplified and fed back to drive the 
crystal at this frequency. Electrical coupling is done with metal 
electrodes deposited on the opposite faces of a thin quartz wafer having 
the proper crystalline orientation. For deposition monitoring, one 
electrode is exposed to the vapor flux and proceeds to accumulate a 
mass of deposit. This mass loading reduces the crystal’s resonant 
frequency, r.
