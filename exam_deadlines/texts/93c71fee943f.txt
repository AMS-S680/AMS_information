Importance of sample preparation
Sample preparation is often considered the "low tech" part of the method, and thus receives little attention. In some laboratories, the youngest, least experienced (and usually lowest paid) workers are assigned to the "Prep Lab." It is assumed that their inexperience will be less of a liability in this part of the organization.
Despite its "low tech" image, sample preparation is a critical part of every method. Although many procedures do not use sophisticated, and expensive, equipment, they still require considerable care in operation to ensure that reliable results are obtained.
All sample preparation procedures are based on sound chemical and physical principles. To successfully, and efficiently, use these procedures, you must understand these principles, and then apply your knowledge of them to the task at hand.

Metal sample preparation for microscopic evaluation
Typically, the grains of a metal object are very small, and cannot be viewed with the naked eye.
The structural features of the small grains are observed using an optical or electron microscope.
Structures requiring this range of magnification for their examination are called microstructures. 
The crystal structure of a solid metal refers to the internal structure or arrangement of the atoms in an ordered, repeating, 3D pattern.
Normal metallic objects are polycrystalline, which means they consist of an aggregate of many very small crystals. These crystals are called grains.

Chemical and Thermal etching
Polished unetched samples can show macroscopic cracks, pits, etc., and no microstructural details can be observed.
Therefore, a so-called etching process is needed to reveal the microstructures.  Etching for microstructural evaluation is to expose the lowest-energy surface by chemical or thermal means.
Etching will expose defects such as grain boundaries and bring out the contrast between different phases or different crystallographic orientations that etch at different rates.

Other special sample preparation methods for surface analysis
In-situ heating
Refractory metals and silicon surfaces can be cleaned sufficiently by flash heating, that is heating the samples to a very high temperature for a very short time whereby the surface oxides becomes unstable and vaporize in vacuum.
In-situ abrasion and scraping
A razor blade or a grinding tool is attached at an appropriate angle to rotating or sliding shafts inside the vacuum system to grind the sample surface.
In-situ cleavage
Some crystalline materials have preferred cleavage planes that can be cleaved easily inside the vacuum chamber using a blade or a rod.

Important terminologies

It is the average distance that a molecule travels in a gas between two successive collisions with other molecule of that gas.

 It is the number of molecules striking the  surface per unit area per unit time

Ideal gas 
vs.
 Real gas
Ideal gas
The molecules are minute spheres.
Their volume is very small compared with that actually occupied by the gas.
The molecules DO NOT exert forces on each other.
The molecules travel along rectilinear paths in a perfectly random fashion.
The molecules make perfectly elastic collisions.
Real gas
Each molecule has certain significant volume which exclude other molecules.
There are intermolecular forces among the molecules.
These interactions lead to phenomenon such as condensation, vaporization, melting, freezing, and etc
Random but inelastic collisions are expected among molecules.

Classification of vacuum
     I.	 
Low (& medium) vacuum
   	The number of molecules in gaseous phase is large compared with that of covering the surfaces, thus in this range the pumping is directed towards rarefying the existing phase.
      II.	 
High vacuum
   	The gas molecules are located principally on surfaces, and the mean free path equals or is greater than the pertinent dimensions of the enclosure.  Therefore the pumping consists in evacuating or capturing the molecules leaving the surface and individually reaching pump.  This 	is the range where particles can travel in vacuum enclosure without colliding with other particles.
     III.	 
Ultra-high vacuum
  	 In this regime, the time to form a monolayer on a clean surface is equal or larger than the usual time for laboratory measurement, thus “clean” surface can be prepared and their properties can be determined before the adsorbed gas layer is form.

=== PAGE 66 ===


=== PAGE 67 ===
a free electron is accelerating towards the anode. When the electron acquires sufficient energy and when it strikes a molecule, it can ionize the molecule and create an electron and a positive ion.  When the electron strikes the anode, it is removed from the process.
in order to increase the pathlength of electrons so that the ionization process is enhanced, the electron is allowed to pass through the hole in the ring positively biased.  The electron will then follow oscillatory motion instead of directly striking the anode.
an additional magnetic field provides a force on the electrons which is perpendicular to the direction of motion and causes the electrons to move in a spiral path.  Thus, the probability for the electron to have collision with other molecules is greatly increased.

=== PAGE 68 ===
Sputtering
	The positive ion is accelerated by the cathode and strikes the surface.  The process knocks out the Ti atoms on the cathode.  The sputtered Ti atoms will deposit in the cell.  Metallic Ti is also exposed on the sputtered surface.
2.	Gettering
	Metallic Ti is very reactive.  The gas molecules adsorb on the surface.
3.	Burial
	The sputtered Ti atoms bury the molecules that already adsorbed on the surface.  This burial is the pumping mechanism for noble gases and other inert molecules.
