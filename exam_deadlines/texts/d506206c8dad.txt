Bohr atomic model
 (by Neil Bohr in 1913)
 electrons are assigned into the orbitals according to certain rules;
 they revolve around the nucleus in discrete orbitals;
 characteristic of any particular electron is more or less well-defined in terms of its orbital depending on their energies.
A schematic representation of the Bohr atom
Quantum mechanical description:
 electrons are quantized; they are in certain orbital; they are  
permitted to have only specific values of energy
.
 to change energy, they must jump from one level to another (jumping down – 
emission
; jumping up –  
absorption
).

Basic facts about bonds
(2 classes: Primary & Secondary)
Primary bonding (3 types): ionic
, 
covalent
, and 
metallic
 bonds.
Ionic 
involves the 
transfer of electrons
 from one type of atom (metallic) to another. This bonding results from the 
electrostatic attraction
 (Coulomb force) between oppositely charged ions.
In 
covalent bonded
 materials, the shared electrons forms 
bonds between pairs of adjacent atoms
. Such bonding can result in molecules with as few as two atoms, and up to those with very large number of atoms in the giant molecules as in polymers.
Metallic bonds
 found only in 
metals
, where the 
bonding electrons are delocalized
 and spread out over the array of atoms.
Secondary bonding (2 types)
 : 
Hydrogen bond 
exists between a bound hydrogen atom and the electrons of adjacent atoms. The 
van der Waals force 
exists between adjacent molecules that have molecular dipoles. (different types of charges at both ends , which may be permanent or induced)

Diamond formation
Most natural diamonds are formed at high temperature and pressure at depths of 140 to 190 kilometers in the Earth's mantle. Carbon-containing minerals provide the carbon source, and the growth occurs over periods from 1 billion to 3.3 billion years (25% to 75% of the age of the Earth)

Metamaterials are artificially engineered materials designed to interact with waves—such as light, sound, or radio signals—in ways that do not occur in nature.
Unlike natural materials, which get their properties from atoms and molecules, metamaterials get their unique powers from their 
microscopic structure
. By shaping and organizing these tiny internal patterns, scientists can force waves to bend, block, or bounce in unusual directions.
