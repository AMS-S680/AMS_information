[Speaker notes]
A most common method to generates characteristic UV emission lines by exciting rare gas atoms via electrical discharge.
Gas Filling: A sealed discharge lamp tube is filled with a high-purity rare gas (e.g., He, Ne, 
Ar
). Helium (He) is the most widely used because its emission lines are sharp, stable, and cover the key energy range for valence electron analysis.
Electrical Discharge Excitation: A high voltage (typically several kilovolts) is applied between two electrodes inside the lamp. This creates an electrical discharge that ionizes the rare gas atoms, promoting their electrons to high-energy excited states.
Photon Emission via Relaxation: When the excited gas atoms relax back to their ground state, they emit photons with discrete energies corresponding to the energy difference between the excited and ground states. These discrete photons form the characteristic UV lines used for UPS.

Definition:
 
Radiation emitted by electrons travelling in a circular orbit at near light speed. Synchrotron radiation occurs in the x-ray and ultraviolet regions of the spectrum. Once considered a dissipative nuisance by accelerator physicists, it can be used to investigate phenomena as diverse as the structures of solids and the interiors of heart vessels
Synchrotron Radiation

UPS Instrumentation-synchrotron radiation
[Speaker notes]
Synchrotron radiation facilities provide a superior 
tunable
 UV source that surpasses gas discharge lamps in performance.
Electron Acceleration: Synchrotrons accelerate electrons to relativistic speeds (close to the speed of light) in a storage ring.
Synchrotron Radiation Generation: When these high-speed electrons are deflected by magnetic fields (dipole or undulator magnets), they emit broadband, tunable electromagnetic radiation that spans from infrared to hard X-rays.
UV Band Selection: By using monochromators (e.g., grazing-incidence gratings) to select the UV spectral region (10–45 eV), a tunable, high-brightness UV beam is obtained.

Ultraviolet photoemission spectroscopy (UPS) is very similar to XPS.  However, instead of an x-ray source, a UV source is used to excite the sample.
Upon UV excitation, photoemission proceeds, leading to ejection of photoelectrons as in XPS.
The UV photons have an energy of about several tens eV, which is not enough to photo-ionize core level electrons.
Electrons of more loosely bound states (e.g. VB in semiconductors; HOMO in organic substances) could  be photo-emitted upon UV illumination.
These photoelectrons can reveal details about the electronic band structure (energy level and crystal momentum) of the sample under study.
UPS Principles

In high resolution UPS analysis, the valence band structure of a compound, a molecule or a crystal structure can be revealed;
the UPS peaks can be assigned to different valence energy levels;
the UPS peaks can be regarded as the fingerprint for a specific compound, molecule or crystal structure;
also, UPS peaks may be used to construct the valence band energy diagram;
usually, the UPS spectra will be compared with theoretical energy levels calculated through computation.

Why XPS not good for study of bands?
XPS indeed can excite valence bands to get information about such as density of states, however, it is not suitable for the study of valence bands, especially for the study of energy dispersion (k dependence).
XPS photon energy is too high, the transition probability for valence bands is very small compared with UV light. 
XPS photon have too large energy which leads the electrons from valence bands with very high E
kin
.  It is hard to analyze spectra with high kinetic energy with high resolution.
